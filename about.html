Our Story, Our Sourcing, and the People Behind the Counter

How North Star Bakery Started

North Star Bakery opened in 2011 in a narrow storefront that had been a dry cleaners for twenty years. Marisol Reyes, our head baker, had spent nine years baking in restaurant kitchens where the ovens ran hot and the bread went out the door faster than it could be made. She wanted the opposite: one oven, one neighborhood, and enough time to let a dough take all the time it needs.

The first year, we baked four loaves a day and most of them went to the same six families. Word spread the way it does in a neighborhood — by someone mentioning it to someone else on the way to the bus stop. Today we bake several hundred loaves a week, and a surprising number of them still go to the same families who were there in year one.

We have grown carefully. When we outgrew the original room, we added a second deck oven next door rather than moving to a bigger space, because we did not want to lose the walk-in corner where the neighborhood stops in on Saturday mornings. It is still the best seat in the house.

Our Sourcing Philosophy

We buy as close to home as we can, and we buy from people we have met. This is partly about quality — flour tastes different depending on the season and the mill — and mostly about the fact that a neighborhood bakery should support the businesses around it.

Our Flour

Our bread and pastry flour comes from Halden Mill, roughly forty miles north. We buy whole-grain and unbleached flours from them, and they mill in small batches, which means the flour in our bread is often only weeks old. We store it in the back room, dry and dark, the way it should be stored, and we write the mill's name and lot date on every bin.

Our Butter

The butter for our croissants, danishes, and all-butter cake comes from Meadowlark Creamery. We take delivery twice a week and use it within a few days, so the croissants on Saturday are made with butter that arrived Wednesday.

Our Fruit, Eggs, and Dairy

Seasonal fruit comes from two farms we visit each year, and from the Brookfield farmers' market on Saturdays. Eggs come from a farm up the valley, and our milk and cream come from a dairy that delivers in reusable glass. When we cannot source something locally we will say so on the board rather than quietly switching suppliers.

Our Commitments

  • We buy local whenever a local option exists, even when it costs more.
  • We bake our gluten-reduced bread in a dedicated gluten-free zone that is cleaned down before every bake.
  • We do not send food we would not serve to our own families, and we give our day-old bread to the Brookfield food pantry.
  • We price by what it actually costs us to make, and we have raised prices only when our own costs went up.

Meet the Bakers

Marisol Reyes, Head Baker and Owner

Marisol founded the bakery and still mixes the first batch of the day herself. She is usually the one behind the counter on Saturday mornings and will happily talk your ear off about hydration rates if you let her.

Dana Whitfield, Pastry Lead

Dana runs the pastry case and decides what is on the seasonal board. If you have ever had a danish with a fruit you had never heard of, Dana found it.

Tom Okafor, Production Baker

Tom handles shaping, scoring, and the early shift, and he is the reason our loaves all come out looking the same. He is also the keeper of the bakery's very serious sourdough starter, which is now more than a decade old.

Priya Raman, Front of House

Priya is the first face you see when you walk in. She knows which families have a new baby, which offices need a Friday breakfast, and who is going to want a whole wheat loaf that is not quite ready yet.

Written summary of the video: Tom folds a batch of sourdough dough in a steel bowl and lets it rest. After the bulk ferment, he divides the dough, shapes each piece into a round, and places them seam-side down in floured baskets so they can proof overnight. In the morning the loaves are turned out onto peel boards, scored with a razor, and slid into a deck oven where steam fills the chamber. The camera follows the loaves as they rise, brown, and cool on wire racks. The clip ends with the finished loaves being bagged and labeled with the day's date.
